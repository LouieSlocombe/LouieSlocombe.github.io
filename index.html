Louie Slocombe

Computational physicist & theoretical chemist

I study how molecules behave, from the quantum motion of a proton to the properties of a material. My current focus is sustainable polymers: connecting their chemistry to how they perform and how they break down.

I’m a Group Leader Research Fellow in the Scanlon Materials Theory Group at the University of Birmingham, working with David Scanlon. I combine molecular simulation, machine learning, and chemical data to investigate these questions.

Current research · Birmingham · Since June 2026

How can we make useful polymers with a better end of life?

Polymers are long molecules whose chemistry and arrangement determine how a material behaves. I’m developing ways to connect that molecular picture to material properties and biodegradability, bringing together experimental evidence, computer simulations, and machine learning. The aim is to help guide the design of more sustainable materials.

Learn from experiments

I develop tools to collect information from papers and recover data from published plots, building datasets that retain the conditions behind each measurement.

Follow molecules in motion

I build and simulate polymer structures to explore how their chains pack, respond to forces, and interact with water. Reaction calculations help investigate routes to breakdown and chemical recycling.

Connect data to predictions

I develop machine-learning methods for polymer properties and biodegradability, with an emphasis on testing predictions and understanding where a model can be trusted.

Research background

My research has moved between physics, chemistry, and biology. Across these fields, I use molecular models and data to ask how small-scale interactions shape larger patterns: an error in DNA copying, the organisation of biochemistry, or the behaviour of a material.

2024–2026
Arizona State University

Exploring the chemistry of life

In Sara Walker’s group at the Beyond Center, I investigated what makes biological chemistry distinctive. I combined machine learning, chemical complexity, and graph theory to explore molecular construction, biochemical reaction networks, and protein folding.

I led the CBR-db paper, developing a curated biochemical reaction resource with collaborators, and worked on AssemblyTheoryTools and algorithms that examine how complex molecules can be built from reusable parts. Other collaborations explored the emergence of molecular handedness in metabolism and the striking consistency of elemental composition across proteins from cellular and viral life. The protein-composition work remains a preprint under review.

Alongside research, I was a Research Lab Manager and Postdoctoral Researcher: I supported the group’s day-to-day work, trained and mentored researchers, and contributed substantially to a successful $1.5 million NASA ROSES grant proposal.

Biochemical origins and proteins · Molecular complexity and assembly theory

2018–2024
University of Surrey

Following quantum effects through DNA

My PhD in Quantum Biology, completed in 2022 with Marco Sacchi and Jim Al-Khalili, asked whether the motion of a proton could change how accurately DNA is copied. In Quantum Effects in DNA Replication Fidelity, I studied proton transfer and quantum tunnelling, including how the surrounding molecular environment affects these processes.

I continued as a Research Fellow in Theoretical Quantum Biology, connecting quantum calculations with simulations of DNA, water, and enzymes. Our work showed why the proteins that separate and copy DNA matter to the explanation: they change the conditions in which unusual base-pair arrangements form and survive.

These questions also took me beyond DNA, into ion-channel transport, DNA damage, and surface chemistry. They continue to inform the multiscale simulation and quantum-dynamics tools I develop today.

Teaching & scientific community

I care about making computational research easier to learn and reproduce. I have managed or supervised 28 scientists, taught scientific computing and computational chemistry, and helped develop teaching on generative AI. At Birmingham, I have led and mentored students applying machine learning to the biodegradability of short polymers through the Reckitt Challenge.

Bringing researchers together is another part of that work. As co-chair of the 2025 Quantum Biology Gordon Research Symposium, I raised $22,000 to support attendance by underrepresented and minority early-career researchers.
