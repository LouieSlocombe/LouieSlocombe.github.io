Biochemical origins & proteins

What patterns connect life’s reaction networks, molecular building blocks, and proteins?

Collaborative research · Arizona State

Life uses a small part of the chemistry that is possible. With collaborators in Sara Walker’s group at Arizona State University, I study patterns in that chemistry: which molecules participate in metabolism, how their reactions connect, and what constraints appear across very different organisms.

My contribution combines chemical data curation, molecular modelling, and software for exploring large collections of molecules and proteins.

A database for biochemical reactions

Before comparing reaction networks, we need chemical records that a computer can interpret consistently. I led the CBR-db paper, published in 2026, with collaborators who helped build a curated resource of biochemical compounds and reactions (Slocombe et al., 2026).

CBR-db brings together information from KEGG and the ATLAS of Biochemistry, repairs problematic molecular and reaction representations, and adds properties for analysis. This provides a foundation for studying metabolism, possible routes to early biochemistry, and molecular complexity.

Why does biological chemistry have a handedness?

Some molecules come in mirror-image forms, like left and right hands. This property is called chirality, and it matters because biological molecules can interact differently with each form.

In a collaboration on the emergence of chirality from metabolism, we investigate how expanding reaction networks can favour particular patterns of chiral molecules. The study connects molecular handedness to the organisation of metabolism, offering hypotheses about how chemical selection developed during life’s origins. The paper is accepted at Nature Communications (Malloy et al., 2025).

What stays consistent across proteins?

A proteome is an organism’s collection of proteins. Our 2026 preprint compares their elemental composition across cellular and viral life. Despite enormous differences in sequences and proteome sizes, we find a surprisingly narrow range of elemental compositions (Benites et al., 2026).

We also examine reconstructed ancestral proteomes and simplified amino-acid alphabets to explore where this pattern may come from. Predicted protein structures help us investigate how changes in the available building blocks could affect folding. This work remains a preprint under review.

For these kinds of studies, I develop workflows around ESMFold, a model that predicts protein structure from an amino-acid sequence, including preparation for molecular simulation and execution on different computing accelerators. These are computational predictions, whose confidence and assumptions need to be assessed.

Data and software

The public CBR-db repository provides the biochemical resource. My protein tools and examples support structure prediction and preparation.
