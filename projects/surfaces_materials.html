Surfaces & energy materials

How do atomic interactions control motion on a surface and a material’s response to light?

Collaborative research · Surrey & Birmingham

The same computational chemistry used to study a molecule can help explain how a material behaves. My collaborations in this area connect atomic-scale calculations with experiments and with tools for assessing energy materials.

Following water one molecule at a time

Graphene and hexagonal boron nitride both form honeycomb sheets, but their chemistry is different. In a 2025 collaboration, we combined experiments tracking molecular motion with electronic-structure calculations to investigate how individual water molecules move on these surfaces (Seiler et al., 2025).

On the studied nickel-supported boron nitride surface, water moves through coupled rotation and translation; on graphene, it makes more distinct hops. The supporting material also changes the interaction. This helps explain why similar-looking surfaces can have different friction and water-transport behaviour.

Related collaborative work examines how alkali-metal atoms adsorb on metal surfaces, connecting the way atoms bind with the measurements used to study their motion (Raghavan et al., 2021).

Assessing materials for solar cells

I contribute to Solphin, a collaborative Python package for analysing whether a material could be useful as a solar absorber. It connects electronic-structure calculations with optical absorption and estimates of the theoretical limits of photovoltaic efficiency (Cox et al., 2026).

These estimates help compare candidate materials; they describe model limits rather than the efficiency of a finished device. The accompanying 2026 manuscript is a preprint, being prepared for submission to the Journal of Open Source Software.

Building models of disordered materials

Many materials do not have a repeating crystal lattice. Through AmorphGen, developed with the Scanlon Materials Theory Group, I contribute to methods for constructing amorphous structures by random placement, simulated melting and cooling, or combinations of these approaches.

Generating several plausible structures lets us explore how much a calculated property depends on the particular atomic arrangement. This complements my work on molecular simulation.

The public projects are Solphin and AmorphGen.
