Proton transfer in DNA

Can the motion of a single proton change how DNA is copied?

Quantum biology · Surrey & collaborators

DNA is copied by matching its molecular letters, or bases, with their partners. A small rearrangement of hydrogen atoms can change those pairing rules. I study whether proton transfer, including quantum tunnelling, can help create such changes and whether they last long enough to matter during replication.

With my collaborators, I combine quantum calculations with simulations of water and enzymes. The central question is not only whether an unusual base pair can form, but what happens to it as the cell handles the DNA.

Mapping the energy landscape

A tautomer is a form of a molecule with a different arrangement of hydrogen atoms and bonds. Our calculations map routes between ordinary DNA base pairs and their tautomers, and the energy barriers along the way. The adenine–thymine and guanine–cytosine pairs behave differently, so an explanation for one cannot simply be applied to the other (Slocombe et al., 2021).

When quantum motion matters

Quantum tunnelling allows a proton to cross a barrier that a classical particle would have to climb over. In our model of guanine–cytosine, tunnelling strongly increases proton transfer even when the base pair exchanges energy with its surroundings (Slocombe et al., 2022).

A calculated tautomer population is only one part of a possible mutation mechanism. Its biological significance depends on the lifetime of that state and on the enzymes that separate and copy the strands.

What happens as the strands separate?

DNA must unzip before it can be copied. We found that stretching the hydrogen bonds changes the reaction landscape: it can slow the formation of a tautomer while also making an existing one harder to reverse (Slocombe et al., 2022). Related work on adenine–thymine shows why the separating strands need to be considered alongside the closed double helix (King et al., 2023).

The enzymes change the answer

The proteins handling DNA are active participants in this chemistry. Our simulations of the PcrA helicase, an enzyme that separates the strands, show that its local environment can strongly suppress guanine–cytosine tautomer formation (Winokan et al., 2023).

In a different setting, the DNA-copying enzyme polymerase can help a guanine–thymine mismatch access a different pairing arrangement through proton transfer (Slocombe et al., 2023). Comparing these cases helps us understand why the same chemical process may have different consequences in different parts of the replication machinery.

Beyond the canonical bases

More recent collaborative work examines O6-methylguanine, a chemically modified base. Methylation disrupts its hydrogen bonding, while nearby water can provide another route for proton transfer (de Abrantes et al., 2025).

We have also investigated hachimoji DNA, a synthetic genetic system with additional letters, to ask how changing the base chemistry affects proton-transfer pathways (Warman et al., 2023). Together, these studies test how far ideas from ordinary DNA extend to modified and synthetic systems.

Methods

I develop simulation tools that connect reaction paths, quantum nuclear motion, and the surrounding molecular environment. Public inputs and analysis for several studies are listed under code behind the papers.

Related threads

Quantum effects do not play the same role in every biological system. Our ASIC1 ion-channel preprint investigates why this membrane protein favours particular ions. Its calculations find extremely short coherence lifetimes and support a classical explanation of selectivity; the manuscript remains under review (Vallée et al., 2025). Our broader review discusses the range of questions studied in quantum biology (Kim et al., 2021).
