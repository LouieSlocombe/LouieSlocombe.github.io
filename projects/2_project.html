Molecular complexity & assembly theory

What can a molecule’s construction tell us about the processes that made it?

Theory, algorithms, & life detection

A molecule’s structure contains clues about the chemistry that produced it. Assembly theory approaches this by asking how many steps are needed to build an object from elementary parts, allowing previously built fragments to be reused.

I work on the practical side of this question: algorithms for finding construction pathways, software for comparing molecules, and connections between the calculations and experimental measurements. Much of this work is a collaboration with the Emergence of Life group at Arizona State University.

Counting construction steps

The assembly index is the fewest joining steps needed under a specified set of rules. It describes a possible minimal construction, rather than reconstructing the exact reactions that happened in the past. The copy number describes how many copies of that object occur in a sample.

Our perspective on measuring molecular complexity discusses how structural calculations and measurements such as mass spectrometry, infrared spectroscopy, and nuclear magnetic resonance can help investigate molecular assembly (Slocombe & Walker, 2024).

Making the calculations practical

Finding the shortest construction becomes difficult as molecules get larger. I work on search algorithms and useful approximations that let us compare more structures. Our 2026 Assembly Spaces preprint develops formal definitions and fast methods for bounding assembly indices; it remains under review (Siebert et al., 2026).

A bound is useful even when the exact answer is out of reach: it tells us a range in which that answer must lie. Keeping track of what is exact and what is an estimate is essential when comparing molecules.

AssemblyTheoryTools

AssemblyTheoryTools provides a common Python interface to assembly calculators for molecules, strings, and graphs. It also helps visualise how fragments combine and compare structures that share building blocks. I lead the associated methods manuscript, which is under review (Slocombe et al., 2026).

Recent collaborative software includes ParallelAssemblyCpp for parallel searches and assemblycfg for fast bounds and reusable-fragment pathways. The assembly code catalogue links to these projects.

Connecting structure and measurement

For an unfamiliar sample, we may have a spectrum before we know every molecular structure. I am interested in how spectral features can be related to assembly measures, and how those estimates compare with calculations on known molecular graphs. Such comparisons help establish what a measurement captures and where an approximation breaks down.

Looking for unfamiliar life

Assembly theory proposes that complex objects found in high abundance can provide evidence of processes that repeatedly select particular construction pathways. Applying this idea to life detection requires checking it against biological and non-biological chemistry, as well as the limits of the measurement.

I contribute to work exploring chemical selection in planetary atmospheres (Janin et al., 2025). Our 2026 Habitable Worlds Observatory white paper proposes how assembly measures could inform exoplanet observations (Walker et al., 2026). This is a developing approach to evaluating biosignatures, rather than a reported detection of life.

Chemical data to test the ideas

Our CBR-db resource provides curated biochemical compounds and reactions for exploring chemical space (Slocombe et al., 2026). It supports comparisons between molecular structure, reaction networks, and complexity.

Related work on biochemical origins and proteins asks how molecular handedness and elemental composition connect to the organisation of life (Malloy et al., 2025; Benites et al., 2026).
